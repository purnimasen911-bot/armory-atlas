Rifles

Long arms with a rifled barrel — spiral grooves that spin the bullet for stability and accuracy. Three operating principles dominate: bolt-action (manual cycling), semi-automatic (self-loading), and lever-action (a finger-lever cycles the action). These six shaped both world wars and the hunting field.

Semi-automatic · Gas piston

AK-47

Soviet Union1947M. Kalashnikov

A long-stroke gas-piston rifle prized for tolerance of mud, sand, and neglect. Loosely machined parts and generous clearances made it cheap to build, and it is frequently cited as the most-produced rifle series in history.

Semi-automatic · Direct impingement

AR-15 / M16

USA1960sEugene Stoner

A modular, lightweight rifle built around an aluminium receiver. The military select-fire M16 and its civilian semi-auto AR-15 descendants share a direct-impingement system that taps gas back to cycle the bolt.

Semi-automatic · Gas piston

FN FAL

Belgium1953FN Herstal

A 7.62×51mm NATO battle rifle adopted by dozens of nations, earning the nickname "the right arm of the Free World" through the Cold War. Heavier recoil than the AK or AR, but effective at longer ranges.

Bolt-action

Lee-Enfield SMLE

United Kingdom1907RSAF Enfield

The Short Magazine Lee-Enfield carried British and Commonwealth troops through both world wars. A ten-round magazine and a famously smooth rear-locking bolt allowed trained riflemen rapid, accurate fire.

Bolt-action

Mauser 98

Germany1898Paul Mauser

The controlled-round-feed bolt action that virtually every later bolt rifle copied. Its strong front-locking lugs and extractor set the pattern for hunting and military bolt guns for a century.

7.92×57mm5-round internal≈4.1 kg600 mm barrel
Lever-action

Winchester Model 1894

USA1894John M. Browning

A finger-lever cycles rounds from a tubular magazine under the barrel. The 1894 was among the first rifles built for smokeless powder and sold in extraordinary numbers as a deer-hunting rifle.
