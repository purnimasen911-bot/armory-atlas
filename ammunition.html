Class 04 · Cartridges

Ammunition

A complete round of ammunition — the cartridge — bundles four things: a bullet (the projectile), a measure of propellant (powder), a primer (the striking-sensitive igniter), and a case that holds it all. Metric names state bullet diameter × case length in millimetres (9×19mm); imperial names mix calibre with year or powder charge (.30-30: .30-inch bore, 30 grains of the original black powder).

Glossary

Reading the numbers

Rimfire vs centerfire

These describe where the primer sits. In rimfire cartridges the priming compound is spun into the hollow rim, and the firing pin crushes the rim itself — cheap, but limited to low pressures, which is why only small rounds like the .22 LR remain rimfire. In centerfire rounds a separate primer cap sits in the centre of the case head; it can be replaced and the case reloaded, and it safely handles the high pressures of modern rifle and pistol rounds.

Velocity and energy — what they mean

Muzzle velocity is how fast the bullet leaves the barrel; faster means a flatter trajectory and less need to compensate for distance. Muzzle energy (in joules) is the kinetic work the bullet carries — roughly, its potential effect on a target — and scales with both mass and the square of speed. A .22 LR and a 5.56mm bullet have nearly the same diameter, but the 5.56 carries well over ten times the energy.

Calibre, gauge, and naming quirks

"Calibre" is bore diameter — .45 means roughly 0.45 inch, 9mm means 9 millimetres — but names are historical, not exact: a .38 Special actually uses a .357-inch bullet. Shotguns use gauge, where the number of lead balls of bore diameter that weigh one pound gives the designation (12-gauge = twelve balls to the pound). Metric rifle names give diameter × case length, so 7.62×39mm and 7.62×51mm share a bullet diameter but the longer case holds far more powder.
